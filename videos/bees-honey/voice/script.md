## Plain lines (paste into ElevenLabs, one file each)

1. This jar of honey took a lot of work: hundreds of bees, millions of flowers, and tens of thousands of kilometres of flying. But flowers don't make honey. Bees do. So how?
2. Meet a forager bee. She visits flower after flower, sipping sugary nectar through her long tongue and storing it in a special second stomach, called a honey stomach, until it's nearly full.
3. Back at the hive, she passes the nectar mouth to mouth to younger house bees. Each one adds enzymes that break the nectar's sugar into simpler sugars, the first step to honey.
4. The house bees spread the drops into wax cells. But nectar is mostly water, around seventy to eighty percent. Honey is under twenty. Somehow, the bees have to get rid of all that water.
5. So they fan it. Thousands of bees beat their wings over the open cells, pushing warm air through the hive, until the nectar thickens into honey, about seventeen or eighteen percent water.
6. When it's ready, they seal each cell with a tiny lid of fresh beeswax, made in glands on their own bodies. Sealed and dry, honey keeps for an astonishingly long time, even years.
7. But why go to all this trouble? Honey is the colony's food for winter, when there are no flowers. A strong hive can make far more than it needs, which is the extra that beekeepers collect.
8. And here's the twist. In her whole life, about five or six busy weeks, one worker bee makes only about a twelfth of a teaspoon of honey. That's it. One tiny drop, from an entire life.
9. So every jar of honey is hundreds of bees' whole lives, stirred together. Next time you spread some on toast, maybe say a little thank you to the bees.
