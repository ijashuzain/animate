## Plain lines (paste into ElevenLabs, one file each)

1. You could never drink the sea: there's 35 grams of salt in every litre. But the rivers that fill it are fresh. So where does the salt come from?
2. First, the evidence. That's about three and a half percent of every glass. Spread all the sea's salt over the land, and it would make a layer about one hundred and sixty-six metres thick.
3. Clue one: the rain. Raindrops pick up a little carbon dioxide from the air, which makes them slightly acidic. Over thousands of years, that weak acid slowly dissolves rock.
4. Clue two: the rivers. They carry those dissolved minerals, like sodium and chloride, all the way to the sea. River water does contain salt. Just far too little for you to taste.
5. Clue three: the sea floor. Deep down, hot vents pour out water loaded with dissolved minerals from the rock below. The whole ocean may pass through these vents every ten to twenty million years.
6. Clue four: the Sun. It lifts water off the sea as vapour, but salt can't evaporate. So the water leaves, falls again as fresh rain, and the salt stays behind, trapped.
7. But then, why isn't the sea getting saltier every year? Because salt leaves too. Some sinks into the mud of the sea floor, and sea life uses some, so the saltiness stays about the same.
8. So here's the answer. The rivers were never really fresh. They have been carrying a pinch of salt to the sea for hundreds of millions of years, and the sea is where the water leaves and the salt stays.
9. So the next time you swallow a wave at the beach, remember what you're really tasting: old mountains, dissolved by rain, carried by rivers, and left behind by the Sun.
