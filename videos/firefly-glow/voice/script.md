## Plain lines (paste into ElevenLabs, one file each)

1. You've seen fireflies glow, but you've never felt one get hot. How do they make light without heat? An old light bulb turns about ninety percent of its power into heat.
2. At dusk, our firefly climbs a blade of grass. Surprise: it isn't a fly at all. Fireflies are beetles, and the light comes from a special organ at the tip of the belly.
3. Inside that lantern, a molecule called luciferin meets an enzyme called luciferase, plus oxygen and a little energy. The reaction releases its energy as light, and very little as heat.
4. To flash, the firefly controls the air that reaches its lantern through tiny breathing tubes. A rush of oxygen, and it glows. Cut the oxygen, and the light goes out.
5. Every species has its own flash code: how many flashes, how long, how far apart. The males fly and flash. A female in the grass answers with her own flash, at just the right moment.
6. Old textbooks said almost every reaction makes a flash. In 2008, scientists measured about four in ten. But even so, a firefly's light is far cooler than a light bulb, which turns most of its power into heat.
7. But glowing in the dark is risky. Bats, frogs and spiders hunt at night. Many fireflies carry bitter chemicals in their blood, so most hunters learn that the light means: don't eat me.
8. And here's the twist. Some female fireflies fake another species' answer. A male flies down, expecting a mate, and gets eaten instead. She even steals his bitter chemicals. Scientists call them femme fatale fireflies.
9. By dawn, the lanterns go dark. Somewhere in the grass, a little beetle rests, ready to light up the night again, with a light that hardly gets warm.
