## Plain lines (paste into ElevenLabs, one file each)

1. Your body has never met this germ. Yet after one small shot, it knows what the germ looks like and how to beat it. How do you train for an enemy you've never met?
2. Germs like viruses and bacteria are covered in unique shapes, called antigens. Think of them as a face. Your immune system learns to recognise that face, and then hunts down anything wearing it.
3. Normally, the first time a germ invades, your body is caught off guard. It can take a week or two to build the right defences. Meanwhile, the germ multiplies, and you get sick.
4. A vaccine skips the danger. It shows your body the germ's face without the real fight: a weakened or killed germ, just a harmless piece of it, or instructions for your own cells to make that piece.
5. Sentinel cells grab that piece and carry it to your lymph nodes, like a wanted poster. There they show it around until they find the immune cells with exactly the right match.
6. Then the match multiplies. B cells pump out antibodies, tiny Y-shaped tags that latch onto the germ's face, and killer T cells destroy infected cells. Your body has built an army for this one enemy.
7. When the job is done, most of that army retires. But some stay behind as memory cells, and they can last for years, even decades. They remember that face, and keep the wanted poster up.
8. Now, if the real germ ever shows up, the memory cells spot it instantly. Your body attacks within days, faster and stronger than before. Often the germ is wiped out before you even feel sick.
9. And it works on a planet-wide scale. Vaccines wiped smallpox off the face of the Earth, the first human disease ever eradicated. And over the last fifty years, immunisation has saved at least one hundred and fifty-four million lives.
10. So a vaccine is a wanted poster, shown to your body before the real criminal arrives. One small shot, and your immune system is ready, for an enemy it has never met.
