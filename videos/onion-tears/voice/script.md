## Plain lines (paste into ElevenLabs, one file each)

1. You've cried over an onion, and you weren't sad. Your eyes were under chemical attack. So why does an onion make you cry, and how do you stop it?
2. An onion is made of millions of tiny cells. Each one keeps two things apart: sulfur chemicals, and enzymes that can change them. Your knife breaks the walls, and the two finally meet.
3. First, an enzyme called alliinase turns the sulfur chemicals into an acid. Then a second enzyme, found in 2002, turns that acid into a gas with a very long name. Try saying it with watery eyes.
4. That gas is light. It drifts up off the cutting board, straight toward the face of the person holding the knife. The closer you lean in, the more of it you meet.
5. When the gas touches your eyes, it irritates the nerves on their surface. Your brain treats it as an attack, and tells your tear glands to wash it away. Tears are your eyes' rinse.
6. Scientists think it's a defence. Onions grow in the ground, where hungry animals dig and bite. One bite breaks the cells, and the attacker gets a face full of stinging gas.
7. Chill the onion first, because cold slows the enzymes down. Use a sharp knife, which breaks fewer cells. And let a fan or an open window carry the gas away from your face.
8. And here's the twist: a tearless onion already exists. In 2018, the Sunion went on sale in America. It took about thirty years of ordinary breeding, choosing the mildest onions again and again.
9. So the next time an onion makes you cry, don't take it personally. It's just a plant protecting itself, one tiny cell at a time. Pass the tissues.
