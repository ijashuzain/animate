## Plain lines (paste into ElevenLabs, one file each)

1. Why does your brain spend about two hours every night making movies you'll never remember? You forget most of them by breakfast, and scientists still argue about the answer.
2. Most vivid dreams happen in REM sleep. Your eyes dart under closed lids, your brain lights up almost as if you were awake, and your body is kept still, so you don't act your dreams out.
3. Idea one: dreams are noise. Signals fire up from the brainstem during REM, and the rest of the brain stitches them into a story, the best it can. Weird plots included.
4. Idea two: dreams help you remember. While you sleep, your brain replays bits of the day and files the important ones away. Sleep's link to memory has some of the strongest support in the lab.
5. Idea three: dreams soften feelings. REM sleep may replay emotional moments with the stress turned down, a kind of overnight therapy. That might be why a bad day can feel smaller in the morning.
6. Idea four: dreams are practice. Being chased, falling, missing a test: one theory says the brain rehearses threats in a safe place, like an ancient survival drill, so you're ready for the real thing.
7. So which idea is right? Honestly, nobody knows for certain. Dreams might do several of these jobs at once, and new studies keep adding pieces to the puzzle.
8. But here's a clue. Newborn babies spend about half their sleep in REM, far more than adults. One idea is that dreaming sleep helps build and wire the brain itself, while it's still growing.
9. So tonight, when your eyes start to dart, your brain gets busy: sorting, soothing, rehearsing, maybe building a little more of itself. You just won't remember most of it. Sweet dreams.
