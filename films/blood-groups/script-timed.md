# Why Do We Have Different Blood Types? Voice script with timing

| # | window | line |

| 1 | 0:00-0:10 | Your blood type is older than humans. And if you're given the wrong one, your body destroys it within minutes. So why don't we all have the same blood? |
| 2 | 0:10-0:16 | To find out, let's follow one red blood cell, for its whole life. |
| 3 | 0:16-0:30 | It's born deep inside a bone, in the soft red marrow. Your marrow makes about two million red cells every second. |
| 4 | 0:30-0:40 | To make room for oxygen, it throws out its own nucleus, and becomes a dimpled disc, about eight thousandths of a millimetre wide. |
| 5 | 0:40-0:56 | Now the important part. Its surface is covered in tiny chains of sugar. Everyone has the same basic chain. But one gene, called ABO, makes an enzyme that adds one last sugar to the tip. |
| 6 | 0:56-1:14 | If your gene makes the A version, it adds one kind of sugar. That's blood group A. The B version adds a different sugar: group B. Inherit one of each from your parents, and you get both: AB. |
| 7 | 1:14-1:26 | And group O? The O gene is missing a single letter, so its enzyme comes out broken. Nothing gets added. O cells keep the bare chain. |
| 8 | 1:26-1:36 | There's a second tag too, a protein called RhD. Have it, and you're positive. Don't, and you're negative. |
| 9 | 1:36-1:50 | Our cell leaves the bone and starts its journey. It goes all the way round your body in about a minute, squeezing through vessels narrower than itself. |
| 10 | 1:50-2:06 | It isn't alone. Floating in your blood are antibodies: Y-shaped proteins that grab anything they don't recognise. And your blood carries antibodies against the sugar tags you don't have. |
| 11 | 2:06-2:20 | Nobody is born with them. They appear in the first months of life, probably because bacteria in the gut carry very similar sugars, and the body learns to fight them. |
| 12 | 2:20-2:40 | That's why the wrong blood is so dangerous. Give group A blood to someone with group O, and their antibodies latch on to every A tag. The cells clump together, and burst. |
| 13 | 2:40-2:52 | So doctors always match blood. O-negative cells carry none of these tags, so in an emergency they can go to almost anyone. |
| 14 | 2:52-3:04 | Now zoom out. You got your ABO gene from your parents, and they got theirs from their parents, back and back and back. |
| 15 | 3:04-3:20 | Past the first humans, about twenty million years, to an ape that was the ancestor of us and of gibbons. Gibbons have A and B too, from the same gene. Our blood types are older than our species. |
| 16 | 3:20-3:40 | So why did all the types survive? The best answer is germs. The malaria parasite clumps A and B cells more easily, and group O seems to get less severe malaria. But cholera tends to hit group O harder. |
| 17 | 3:40-3:52 | Each germ favours a different type, so no type wins everywhere, and the mix is kept alive. The variety itself is the point. |
| 18 | 3:52-4:04 | And our cell? After about 120 days, it's recycled, and two million new ones take its place, every second. All carrying the tags that your ancestors carried, 20 million years ago. |
