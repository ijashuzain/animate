**Description:**
Why are there different blood types, and what are blood groups for? If we all share the same ancestors, why don't we all have the same blood? We follow one red blood cell through its whole life to find out: born in the bone marrow, tagged with a tiny sugar that decides its blood group, guarded by antibodies, and, 20 million years back, shared with gibbons.

Every red cell is covered in chains of sugar. One gene, ABO, makes an enzyme that adds a last sugar to their tips: one kind for group A, another for group B, both for AB. In group O, a single missing letter in the gene breaks the enzyme, so nothing is added. A second tag, the RhD protein, makes you positive or negative. Your blood carries antibodies against the tags you don't have, which is why the wrong transfusion makes cells clump and burst, and why O-negative blood can go to almost anyone. The A/B variation is at least about 20 million years old and shared with gibbons; the best explanation for why all the types survived is germs such as malaria and cholera, which each favour different groups.

**Sources:**
- ABO is a trans-species polymorphism shared by descent with gibbons, at least ~20 million years old: Ségurel et al., PNAS 2012 (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3494955); Science News "Human blood types have deep evolutionary roots"
- The ABO gene (chromosome 9) encodes a glycosyltransferase: A adds N-acetylgalactosamine, B adds galactose to the H antigen; the O allele's single-base deletion makes an inactive enzyme: OMIM 110300; "ABO (gene)", Wikipedia
- Anti-A/anti-B appear at about 3-6 months, probably triggered by gut bacteria: review in PMC7449879; University of Alberta transfusion medicine module
- Group O and less severe malaria (rosetting prefers A and B cells): Rowe et al. 2007, PNAS (PMC2077280); 2023 genotype study (PMC10522014); results across studies are mixed
- Group O and more severe cholera: Harris & LaRocque review, PMC4973168
- Bone marrow makes about 2 million red cells per second; red cells live about 100-120 days: standard physiology texts
