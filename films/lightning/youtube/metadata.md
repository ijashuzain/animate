**Description:**
How does lightning form? A flash over a farm, nine seconds to the bang, and we fly into the storm to watch a thundercloud build its bolt: warm air rising ten kilometres high, ice crystals and soft hail colliding, the cloud splitting into charged layers, and one strike slowed down a thousand times.

Inside a thunderstorm, the updraft throws light ice crystals against heavier graupel (soft hail) again and again; each bump moves a little charge, so the top of the cloud ends up positive and the base negative, and the ground below turns positive. When the difference is big enough, a faint stepped leader feels its way down, streamers rise from the tallest things on the ground, and when they touch, the bright return stroke races up the channel at about a third of the speed of light. The air heats so fast it bursts outward: thunder. And since sound travels about a kilometre every three seconds, nine seconds means the strike was about three kilometres away.

**Sources:**
- Charge separation by collisions of ice crystals and graupel in the updraft; light positive crystals rise, heavier negative graupel sinks (the mechanism is still studied and debated): AMS Glossary of Meteorology, "Thunderstorm charge"; NOAA/NSSL "Lightning basics"
- Stepped leader and upward streamers; the visible return stroke travels upward: NWS "Lightning science: return stroke"; NOAA/NSSL
- Return stroke about 30,000 amperes on average, travelling at about a third of the speed of light; the channel heats to around 30,000 °C (about 50,000 °F): NWS / NOAA lightning facts
- Re-strikes (dart leaders) along the same channel make a flash flicker: NWS slow-motion lightning explainers
- Sound travels about 1 km every 3 seconds (stated from knowledge; hedged "about")
