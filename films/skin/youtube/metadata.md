**Title:** Why Do Humans Have Different Skin Colours?
Alternatives: "Your Skin Is a Map of Your Ancestors' Sun" · "Why We Don't All Have the Same Skin Colour"

**Description:**
Why do people have different skin colours if we all come from the same ancestors in Africa? The answer is written in sunlight. We follow our ancestors on the great walk out of Africa, with a UV gauge in the sky and cutaways into the skin, to see how melanin, folate and vitamin D shaped the colour of human skin.

After our ancestors lost most of their body hair, more than a million years ago, dark skin protected them from the strong African sun: pigment cells called melanocytes hand melanin to skin cells, which stack it like an umbrella over their DNA. Ultraviolet light can damage DNA and breaks down folate, a vitamin needed for healthy babies, but it also makes vitamin D in the skin. So skin is a balance. As people spread out of Africa, around 60,000 years ago, into places with a lower, weaker sun, lighter skin slowly became an advantage. It evolved separately in Europe and East Asia through different genes, and later than you might think. The Arctic is an exception, Africa itself holds one of the widest ranges of skin colour, and a sunlight map and a skin-colour map line up remarkably well. Skin colour comes down to a handful of genes: underneath, we are one family.
