# Why Do Humans Have Different Skin Colours? Voice script with timing

Record after the picture (ElevenLabs). Each line should fit its window; the picture will be re-fitted to your recording,
so your natural pace is fine. Calm, warm, curious. About 4:00.

| # | window | line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0:00-0:12 | Every person alive today comes from the same ancestors in Africa. So why don't we all have the same skin colour? The answer is written in sunlight. |
| 2 | 0:12-0:24 | Go back more than a million years. Our ancestors lost most of their body hair, probably to stay cool while walking and running in the heat. |
| 3 | 0:24-0:32 | Bare skin under the African sun needed protection. So their skin became dark. |
| 4 | 0:32-0:46 | Inside the skin, special cells called melanocytes make a pigment, melanin. They pack it into tiny parcels and hand them to the skin cells around them. |
| 5 | 0:46-0:54 | Each skin cell stacks the melanin over its DNA, like an umbrella. |
| 6 | 0:54-1:06 | Ultraviolet light can damage DNA. And it breaks down folate, a vitamin the body needs for healthy babies. |
| 7 | 1:06-1:16 | But sunlight also does good: in the skin, UV makes vitamin D, which we need for strong bones. |
| 8 | 1:16-1:26 | So skin is a balance: enough melanin to protect folate, not so much that it blocks vitamin D. |
| 9 | 1:26-1:38 | Somewhere around 60,000 years ago, small groups began to walk out of Africa. |
| 10 | 1:38-1:50 | Not in one trip: over thousands of generations, along coasts, across mountains, into new lands. |
| 11 | 1:50-2:02 | The further they went from the equator, the weaker and lower the sun, especially in winter. |
| 12 | 2:02-2:12 | There, dark skin blocked much of the little UV left, and making enough vitamin D got harder. |
| 13 | 2:12-2:24 | Over many generations, people with slightly lighter skin had a small advantage, and passed it on. |
| 14 | 2:24-2:36 | In Europe and in East Asia, lighter skin evolved separately, through changes in different genes. |
| 15 | 2:36-2:48 | And later than you might think: ancient DNA suggests that many early Europeans still had dark skin until roughly 8 to 10 thousand years ago. |
| 16 | 2:48-3:00 | The Arctic is an exception: the Inuit kept fairly dark skin, getting vitamin D from fish and seal instead of sunlight. |
| 17 | 3:00-3:12 | Africa itself holds one of the widest ranges of skin colour anywhere, from very dark to quite light. |
| 18 | 3:12-3:22 | And groups that settled under strong sun again, such as in South Asia, kept or regained darker skin. |
| 19 | 3:22-3:36 | Put a map of sunlight next to a map of skin colour, and they line up remarkably well. |
| 20 | 3:36-3:50 | Skin colour comes down to a handful of genes answering the sun: a tiny part of our DNA. Underneath, we are one family. |
| 21 | 3:50-4:00 | Your skin is a map of where your ancestors lived, and how strong their sun was. |
