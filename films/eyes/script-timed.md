# How Your Eyes Turn Light Into a Picture: voice script with timing

Record after the picture (ElevenLabs). The picture is re-fitted to your recording, so your natural pace is fine.
Curious, warm, a little wonder. About 4:00.

| # | window | line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0:00-0:12 | Look at this apple. To you it just looks red. But in the moment before you saw it, a ray of light made an incredible journey. Let's ride along. |
| 2 | 0:12-0:22 | It starts at the Sun. Sunlight is a mix of all colours. The apple's skin soaks up most of them, and bounces back the red. |
| 3 | 0:22-0:32 | One ray of red light flies off the apple, crosses the room in a few billionths of a second, and hits your eye. |
| 4 | 0:32-0:44 | First, the cornea: the clear dome at the front. It does most of the focusing, about two thirds, bending the light inward. |
| 5 | 0:44-0:54 | Then the pupil: the black hole in the coloured iris. In bright light, muscles shrink it. In the dark, they open it wide. |
| 6 | 0:54-1:06 | Behind it, the lens fine-tunes the focus. Look at something close, and tiny muscles let it bulge rounder. |
| 7 | 1:06-1:16 | Our ray crosses the clear jelly that fills the eyeball, and lands at the back, on the retina. And the picture there is upside down. |
| 8 | 1:16-1:30 | The retina is built backwards. Light has to pass through layers of nerve wiring before it reaches the sensors at the very back. |
| 9 | 1:30-1:44 | There are two kinds of sensors: about 120 million rods, for dim light, and about 6 million cones, for colour and detail. |
| 10 | 1:44-1:56 | Cones come in three types, tuned to reds, greens and blues. Our red ray finds a red-sensitive cone. |
| 11 | 1:56-2:10 | Inside, it hits one molecule, called retinal. In less than a trillionth of a second, the light snaps it straight. This is the very first step of seeing. |
| 12 | 2:10-2:22 | That tiny change sets off a chain reaction, and the cone fires an electrical signal. |
| 13 | 2:22-2:34 | Nerve cells in the retina collect the signals and send them out through the optic nerve, a cable of about a million fibres. |
| 14 | 2:34-2:46 | Where the cable leaves the eye, there are no sensors at all. Each eye has a blind spot, and you never notice, because your brain fills in the gap. |
| 15 | 2:46-2:58 | The two optic nerves meet at a crossing. Signals from the left side of your view go to the right side of your brain, and the other way round. |
| 16 | 2:58-3:12 | Then they race to the back of your head, to the visual cortex, in less than a tenth of a second. |
| 17 | 3:12-3:26 | And the upside-down picture? Your brain never sees a picture at all. It reads patterns of signals, and builds the scene the right way up. |
| 18 | 3:26-3:40 | Edges, colours, shapes and movement are worked out separately, then put back together into one view. |
| 19 | 3:40-3:52 | And all of that happens again and again, for millions of rays, every moment your eyes are open. |
| 20 | 3:52-4:00 | So you don't really see with your eyes. You see with your brain. Your eyes are just where the light gets in. |
