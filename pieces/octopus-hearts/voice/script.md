## Plain script (for pasting into ElevenLabs)

1. This octopus has three hearts.
2. You have one. So why does an octopus need three?
3. Because its blood is blue.
4. Instead of iron, it carries oxygen with copper, in a protein called hemocyanin.
5. Copper blood holds less oxygen than ours,
6. and it's thicker, so it's harder to push around.
7. And every drop has to squeeze through the gills to pick up oxygen.
8. So the octopus has two extra hearts, one beside each gill, to pump blood through them.
9. Then the third heart, the big one, sends that fresh blood around the whole body,
10. to eight arms, and a very big brain.
11. But when an octopus swims, that body heart briefly stops.
12. That's why octopuses would rather crawl.
13. Three hearts. Two for the gills. One for everything else.

Pronunciation: **hemocyanin** = "HEE-moh-SY-uh-nin". If ElevenLabs gets it wrong, try spelling it "heemo-sigh-a-nin".

## Facts and sources

- Two branchial (gill) hearts and one systemic (body) heart; copper-based haemocyanin carries oxygen less efficiently than haemoglobin. Source: [BBC Science Focus](https://www.sciencefocus.com/nature/why-does-an-octopus-have-more-than-one-heart)
- Haemocyanin floats freely in the blood (not inside cells), so a higher concentration would make the blood too thick. That limits how much oxygen it can carry. Source: cephalopod physiology papers ([Melzner et al. 2007](https://epic2-clone.awi.de/16715/1/Mel2007a.pdf), [Seibel, vampire squid blood](https://pure.lib.usf.edu/en/publications/vampire-blood-respiratory-physiology-of-the-vampire-squid-cephalo/))
- The systemic heart stops briefly during jet-propelled swimming, as measured in a 1987 *Journal of Experimental Biology* study. Source: [summary](https://scienceblog.com/t-octopus-three-hearts-jet-swimming-systemic-heart-stops/). The script says "briefly" on purpose: popular claims that it stops for the whole swim overstate the evidence.
