## Plain lines (paste into ElevenLabs, one file each)

1. Look up on a clear day, and the sky is blue. But at sunset, the very same sky turns orange, pink and red. Same sky, same Sun. So what's really going on up there?
2. Sunlight looks white, but it's really every colour of the rainbow mixed together. Shine it through a glass prism and it fans out into red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet, just as Isaac Newton showed.
3. Each colour travels as a wave. Red light has long, lazy waves, about seven hundred billionths of a metre apart. Blue light has short, choppy ones, closer to four hundred and fifty.
4. Our air is made of tiny molecules, mostly nitrogen and oxygen, far smaller than those waves. They scatter short waves much more than long ones. So blue light gets bounced all over the sky, while red mostly passes straight through.
5. That scattered blue light reaches your eyes from every direction at once, not just from the Sun. So wherever you look, the whole sky seems to glow blue.
6. But violet waves are even shorter, so they scatter even more. Why isn't the sky violet, then? The Sun sends out less violet light, and our eyes are less sensitive to it. So we see blue.
7. At sunset, the light has to skim through much more air to reach you. On that long trip, most of the blue is scattered away, out of sight. What's left are the reds and oranges that paint the evening sky.
8. And on Mars, it's the other way around. Fine red dust in its thin air turns the daytime sky a butterscotch colour, and lets blue light through near the Sun, so Martian sunsets glow blue.
9. So next time you look up, remember: the blue sky is sunlight, bouncing off the air all around you. And every red sunset is that same light, after a very long trip.
